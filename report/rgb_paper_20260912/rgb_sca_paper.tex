\begin{abstract}
Power side-channel attacks have been shown to recover \emph{grayscale} inputs to FPGA
convolutional neural network accelerators. Both foundational results---Wei
et~al.~(ACSAC'18) and Huegle et~al.~(FCCM'23)---deliberately set the first layer to a
single input channel, so whether colour survives the first-layer accumulation has never
been tested. We build an RGB first-layer accelerator on a CW305 Artix-7, capture 12{,}033
power traces with a ChipWhisperer-Lite across three channel dataflows, and evaluate reconstruction with a control set
absent from the prior literature: an analytic prior-only baseline, a shuffled-trace
control, a channel-permutation penalty, and a luminance/chrominance decomposition.
We recover recognisable image structure from measured traces (MSSIM $0.565$, MAE
advantage $+14.19$ over the optimal constant predictor) while recovering
\emph{no colour}. This holds across \emph{all three} channel dataflows---
accumulator-only, per-product-registered, and channel-serial---so the negative is a
property of the leakage at this signal-to-noise ratio rather than of one architectural
choice: chrominance advantage is negative in ten of twelve per-channel measurements,
and on the group that actually reconstructs the channel-permutation penalty is at or
below zero once the no-input prior's own penalty is subtracted.
Rather than report a pooled point score, we decompose recovery into per-task
split-conformal bounds calibrated against that same no-input prior. This bounds the
negative instead of merely asserting it. Luminance sets are $20$--$53\%$ tighter than
the prior in all three dataflows and stay below it under every re-split of the
calibration set. No chrominance interval separates from the prior in any of six
population-by-dataflow cells: the tightest runs from $7\%$ tighter to $16\%$ looser, and
under channel-serial chrominance is resolvably \emph{worse}.
Because the tinted inputs carry per-image random chroma, this negative cannot be
explained by a model colourising from the dataset prior. We further show the failure on
natural photographs is driven by scene complexity rather than colour---grayscale CIFAR-10
fails identically---and quantify the deficiency: natural-image recovery requires
$5.8\times$ more signal-to-noise than this bench provides, of which averaging supplies a
measured $1.70\times$. We report this as \emph{grayscale recovery from an RGB-input
accelerator}, explicitly not RGB reconstruction, and argue the controls and the
calibrated per-task reporting are the transferable contribution. The calibrated
analysis is retrospective and labelled as such.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Wei et~al.~\cite{wei2018} recovered MNIST digits from the power consumption of a
line-buffer CNN accelerator, and Huegle et~al.~\cite{huegle2023} recovered MNIST and
Fashion-MNIST using a generative CNN trained on on-chip voltage traces. Both are
grayscale by construction. Wei et~al.\ state their modification explicitly---``input
channel to 1'' (\S6.3)---and Huegle et~al.\ reduce ``the input channels to a single
channel'' (\S III-B) because both evaluated datasets are grayscale. Wei et~al.\ name
multi-channel colour inputs as untested future work (Appendix~B).

The question this leaves open is not cosmetic. A first-layer convolution sums over input
channels: for a $3\times3$ kernel, 27 input values produce one accumulator value. Whether
per-channel contributions remain observable is an architecture-dependent property, and
answering it decides whether the demonstrated privacy risk extends from grayscale
medical or handwriting imagery to colour photography.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item An RGB first-layer accelerator on CW305 with \emph{no leakage amplifier} and a
runtime-selectable channel dataflow, so one bitstream measures all conditions.
\item A control set that separates recovery from dataset memorisation: an analytic
prior-only baseline, a shuffled-trace control, a channel-permutation penalty, and a
luma/chroma decomposition. None of these appears in the prior work.
\item A measured negative on colour under conditions that exclude the two obvious
confounds (scene complexity, and colourisation from the prior).
\item A quantified instrumentation requirement derived from hardware, not modelling.
\item A per-task calibrated reporting formulation (\S\ref{sec:calibrated}) that
converts a pooled point score into bounds that can be withheld task by task, each
against the no-input prior calibrated identically. It is what turns ``no colour'' from
an assertion into a measured ceiling, and it reports how much of its own verdict table
is an artifact of the calibration split.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Input recovery from FPGA accelerator power.} Wei et~al.~\cite{wei2018} attack
a binarised CNN on a Spartan-6 (SAKURA-G) with an external $2.5$\,GS/s oscilloscope,
recovering MNIST digits by thresholding per-cycle power into foreground/background
(\S6) and by a power template over a $3\times3$ patch (\S7), reporting $89.8\%$
recognition accuracy for a $3\times3$ kernel. Moini et~al.~\cite{moini2021} move the
measurement on-chip, using TDC sensors on local Xilinx boards and AWS F1 instances, and
report a maximum normalised cross-correlation of $79\%$ locally and $72\%$ on F1---but
average thousands of traces per image (their Fashion-MNIST experiment uses $6{,}000$
runs) and post-process with a high-pass filter and total-variation denoising. Huegle
et~al.~\cite{huegle2023} replace the hand-built template with a generative CNN trained
by regression on TDC traces, reach $96.4\%$ recognition on MNIST, and criticise
\cite{moini2021} precisely for its averaging cost and for not reporting recognition or
pixel-level metrics.

All three are grayscale, and not incidentally: \cite{wei2018} sets ``input channel to
1'' (\S6.3) and \cite{huegle2023} reduces ``the input channels to a single channel''
(\S III-B). \cite{moini2021} evaluates only MNIST and, in a limitations experiment,
Fashion-MNIST. Wei et~al.\ list multi-channel colour inputs as future work
(Appendix~B). To our knowledge no published work recovers colour inputs from an FPGA
accelerator power side-channel, and the recent survey of application-specific power
side-channel attacks~\cite{sanjaya2024} lists no such entry in its input-recovery
table.

\paragraph{Colour recovery by other mechanisms.} Maji et~al.~\cite{maji2021} \emph{do}
recover CIFAR-10 and ImageNet inputs---colour images---reporting exact recovery. The
mechanism differs in a way that matters here: the platform is a microcontroller
(ATmega328P, Cortex-M0+, RISC-V) and the channel is principally \emph{timing}, of
floating-point multiplication and integer-to-float conversion, plus SPA on the
conditional subtractions inside a fixed-point normalisation divide. Each input operand
passes through its own instruction, so per-channel values never merge. That is the
opposite of the situation studied here, where one accumulation absorbs all channels.
Wang et~al.~\cite{wang2023} reconstruct MRI inputs to a U-Net on an analog
compute-in-memory accelerator using a GAN; the imagery is grayscale, and we were not
able to determine from the abstract whether the power data is measured or simulated.

\paragraph{Evaluation practice.} The metrics used above---recognition accuracy, MSSIM,
pixel-level distance---measure different things, and both foundational papers show it.
Wei et~al.'s Fig.~8 reports pixel-level distances of $1.65$ and $2.98$, which they
describe as both close to the original, alongside recognition accuracies of $89.8\%$ and
$15\%$. Huegle et~al.\ observe the converse (\S V-A): worse pixel-level distance than
\cite{wei2018} but significantly higher recognition. Neither reports a no-input baseline,
so neither separates ``recovered the input'' from ``reproduced the dataset''---the gap
this work's control set addresses. Recent deep-learning side-channel work argues for
operating on raw traces rather than hand-engineered features~\cite{sanjaya2024}; we
tested a learned temporal filter against our measured sample window and found it
\emph{worse} (\S6), which we read as evidence that featurisation is not the binding
constraint here.

\section{Threat model and setup}

We assume the adversary knows the accelerator architecture, can profile a comparable
instance with known images, and reconstructs on a host---the same assumptions as
\cite{wei2018,huegle2023}. Measurement is external (ChipWhisperer-Lite across the CW305
shunt), matching \cite{wei2018} rather than the on-chip TDC sensors of \cite{huegle2023}.

\paragraph{Victim.} A $3\times3$ RGB first-layer convolution with binarised $\pm1$
weights, implemented as a line buffer---the structure Wei et~al.\ identify as ``the
actual attack target.'' The image is held in block RAM and streamed one RGBX pixel per
window advance; two delay lines and the $3\times3$ window live in shift registers. The
design occupies 1{,}511 LUTs ($2.4\%$ of an XC7A100T) with \textbf{no leakage
amplifier}---a single registered sink, no dedicated toggling lanes. An earlier
random-access variant that fed 27 taps from distributed RAM required 115\% of the part
and was discarded.

\paragraph{Dataflow as a runtime variable.} Channel dataflow is selected by register, not
by bitstream: \textsc{summed} registers only the accumulator, \textsc{parallel} registers
all 27 products, \textsc{serial} gives each channel its own dwell period. All three
compute identical outputs; only registered state differs. This is deliberate---on this
board, reprogramming over a configured FPGA silently fails, and a per-condition rebuild
previously produced four captures of the same bitstream in unrelated work.

\paragraph{Capture.} $32\times32$ RGB, 12{,}033 traces (4{,}011 per dataflow) at
$5$\,MHz with $4$ ADC samples per cycle. A full scan exceeds the ChipWhisperer-Lite FIFO,
so the core scans the whole image---keeping the line buffer coherent---and frames only
the trigger over a position range; scans are reassembled from 2 segments
(\textsc{summed}) or 5 (\textsc{serial}). Before any trace is retained the design answers
a fixed signature page and every mode is checked against a golden convolution on a
\emph{natural} image; a solid patch would pass trivially without exercising the line
buffer. Table~\ref{tab:prov} records provenance.

\section{Evaluation protocol}

Three output channels prove nothing: that is a property of the final transposed
convolution, not of the trace. We therefore run three arms and never report a headline
number alone.

\textbf{Prior-only} is the training-set mean image, computed analytically. With no
per-sample input a model can emit only a constant, and the MSE-optimal constant is that
mean, so training cannot beat it. We stress this because our first implementation
\emph{trained} this baseline on a constant input: zero feature variance made the network
untrainable, it scored MAE 122 where the optimum is 54, and it inflated the apparent
advantage by ${\sim}68$. Any degenerate-input control must be checked against its
analytic value.

\textbf{Shuffled} pairs real traces with wrong images, preserving all marginals and
destroying only the association. It should match prior-only.

\textbf{Channel permutation} scores the reconstruction against R$\leftrightarrow$B-swapped
truth. If the error does not rise, channel identity was not recovered regardless of the
pooled score.

\textbf{Luma/chroma decomposition.} Natural colour is strongly predictable from luminance
and semantics, so a model can recover structure and \emph{colourise} it from the training
prior---scoring well on pooled PSNR while carrying no colour information. We therefore
report errors per channel and split into $Y$ and $C_bC_r$, never pooled alone.

\paragraph{Content groups.} \emph{Natural}: 2{,}000 CIFAR-10 photographs, untinted.
\emph{Control}: 2{,}000 Fashion-MNIST images upscaled to $32\times32$ with a
\textbf{random per-image RGB tint}---spatial difficulty matched to a configuration known
to reconstruct, while chroma is unpredictable from structure or prior, closing the
colourisation escape route. \emph{Diagnostic}: solid patches and colour bars, excluded
from training, exposing any swapped or dead channel. Groups are trained separately;
pooling would let the easy group carry the hard one.

\paragraph{Structure recovers.} On the control group the generator reaches MSSIM $0.565$
and an advantage of $+14.19$ MAE over the analytic prior (Table~\ref{tab:hw}). Figure~\ref{fig:grid} shows recognisable garment silhouettes
recovered from physically measured power.

\paragraph{Colour does not, in any dataflow.} On the control group---the only group that
reconstructs at all---the channel-permutation penalty is $-0.23$, $-0.13$ and $+0.07$ for
summed, parallel and serial: scoring against channel-swapped truth is \emph{no worse}
than the correct pairing. Since the tints are random per image, chroma is unpredictable
from structure or prior; this is a controlled negative, not a modelling artifact.
\S\ref{sec:calibrated} puts a number on how large any residual colour recovery could
be and still be consistent with these measurements.

The swap penalty must be read against the no-input prior, not in isolation. A constant
predictor incurs one whenever the dataset's channel distributions differ: on CIFAR the
training-mean image alone scores $+1.05$, which accounts for roughly two thirds of the
$+1.58$ the natural-group reconstruction scores. Table~\ref{tab:hw} therefore reports the
\emph{excess} over the prior. On the control group the prior's penalty is $+0.005$---the
random tints equalise the channel means, making the prior swap-invariant by
construction---so there the raw and excess figures coincide, and both are at or below
zero.

That it holds across all three dataflows matters. \textsc{Summed} registers only the
accumulator, \textsc{parallel} registers all 27 products, and \textsc{serial} gives each
channel its own dwell period---three genuinely different sets of registered state,
spanning the plausible design space for this layer. \textsc{Parallel} and \textsc{summed}
are statistically indistinguishable (paired difference $-0.36$, 95\% CI
$[-0.91, +0.16]$; Table~\ref{tab:paired}). The negative is therefore a
property of the channel at this SNR, not an artifact of one architecture.

\paragraph{Natural photographs fail, and not because of colour.} Natural CIFAR-10 scores
below the prior (MSSIM $0.057$). Running the identical pipeline on CIFAR
\emph{luminance} gives MSSIM $0.077$---indistinguishable. The limit is scene complexity.
The calibrated analysis corroborates this from a second direction: natural-group
luminance is supported in $5\%$ of re-splits, and $8\times8$ block-averaged luminance---
the easiest target a reconstructor has---scores \emph{worse} still, in every dataflow
(\S\ref{sec:calibrated}). Notably, neither prior work attempted natural imagery either.

\paragraph{Serial did not help, contradicting simulation.} On hardware \textsc{serial} is
worse than \textsc{summed} and \textsc{parallel} ($+11.73$ vs $+14.19$ and $+14.55$). The
likely cause is that serial spreads identical work over three times the cycles, lowering
per-cycle signal. The paired per-image difference resolves this ordering
(Table~\ref{tab:paired}); \textsc{summed} versus \textsc{parallel} it does not.

Our zero-noise simulation had predicted the opposite ordering. That prediction is
withdrawn: the simulator's serial accumulator was defective (\S\ref{sec:corrections}) and
the Phase~0 pilot split augmented copies of the same source scenes across train and test,
so it measured within-scene memorisation rather than generalisation. Correcting both
inverts the simulated ranking (Table~\ref{tab:phase0}). We report the hardware result and
treat the simulation as non-predictive for dataflow ranking.

\subsection{Uncertainty}
\label{sec:unc}

Every figure above is a mean over a 200-image test set, which says nothing about whether
a difference between two conditions is real. We therefore rescored the frozen
predictions---no retraining---with the \emph{image} as the unit of analysis.

The unit matters. A $32\times32$ RGB image holds $3{,}072$ values, but they are not
$3{,}072$ independent observations of the method: neighbouring pixels are correlated and
all of them share one trace, one scene and one capture. Resampling pixels would shrink
every interval by roughly $\sqrt{3072}$ and manufacture significance. All intervals below
are $10{,}000$-sample percentile bootstraps over images.

Three consequences (Tables~\ref{tab:unc},~\ref{tab:paired}).

\textbf{The structure result is comfortable.} On the control group the advantage is
$+14.19$ $[+12.99, +15.35]$ for \textsc{summed}, well clear of zero.

\textbf{The colour negative is now statistically supported, not merely a point
estimate.} Control-group chrominance advantage is $-0.34$ $[-0.51, -0.18]$ for
\textsc{summed} and $-2.18$ $[-2.82, -1.57]$ for \textsc{serial}: intervals that exclude
zero \emph{on the negative side}. The reconstruction is measurably worse than the
constant predictor at \emph{mean}
chrominance error, not merely no better. This is a statement about the mean; at the
$90$th percentile the two are indistinguishable (\S\ref{sec:calibrated}). We keep both
and do not let the weaker one borrow the strength of the stronger.

\textbf{One dataflow ordering survives and one does not.} \textsc{Serial} is genuinely
worse than both others on the control group ($+2.46$ $[+1.60, +3.31]$ against
\textsc{summed}; $+2.82$ $[+1.94, +3.71]$ against \textsc{parallel}). But
\textsc{summed} versus \textsc{parallel} is $-0.36$ $[-0.91, +0.16]$---unresolved, so we
do not rank them. On the natural group no pair is resolved, which is consistent with all
three failing there for the same reason.

\subsection{Calibrated per-task validation}
\label{sec:calibrated}

Everything above reports a mean. A mean answers ``how well on average,'' which is not the
question a defender asks: they ask what can be bounded for a \emph{new} image, and for
which property of it. We therefore rescored the frozen predictions a second time,
decomposing the pooled score into separate tasks and attaching a calibrated error bound
to each. Nothing was retrained, no trace was reloaded, and no predicted pixel changed---
only the reporting formulation.

\paragraph{Method.} For a task $h_t$ the per-example score is
$e_{i,t} = (255/d_t)\lVert h_t(\hat{x}_i) - h_t(x_i)\rVert_1$, in the same $0$--$255$
units as every MAE above. Split-conformal calibration on a scene-disjoint half gives a
radius $q_t = e_{(k),t}$ with $k = \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$, covering a new
exchangeable example's task error with probability at least $1-\alpha$. Primary tasks are
full-resolution $Y$, $C_b$ and $C_r$, kept separate because averaging $C_b$ with $C_r$
can hide one channel improving while the other degrades. An $8\times8$ area-averaged $Y$
is a secondary diagnostic and never a fidelity claim: a method can score well on block
means and be wrong at every pixel. We use $\alpha = 0.10$ \emph{per task}; three 90\%
statements are not a joint 90\% statement and we do not combine them. The analytic prior
is calibrated by the identical protocol, because a radius alone is uninterpretable---a
radius of $255$ covers every image. A task is supported only if its interval covers
\emph{and} is tighter than the prior's.

\paragraph{Recovering what each row denoted.} This was a prerequisite, not a formality.
The exports store group-local row positions, not source identifiers, and equal positions
in two dataflows do not imply equal images. We recovered identity twice independently---
by replaying the selection arithmetic, and by hashing each exported reference image
against the capture set---and required the two to agree. They agree on all $1{,}200$
rows, with no failed hash lookup and no collision among the $4{,}011$ captured images.

\paragraph{Luminance is supported, in every dataflow.} The control-group $Y$ radius is
$32.1$ against the prior's $51.8$ for \textsc{summed}, $27.4$ for \textsc{parallel} and
$35.4$ for \textsc{serial} (Table~\ref{tab:calibrated})---ratios of $0.62$, $0.53$ and
$0.68$. As an operational statement: $90\%$ of new control images have luminance error at
most ${\sim}32$ units, where a no-input adversary must accept ${\sim}52$. These are the
only three rows in the table whose ratio interval stays below $1$ across every re-split.

\paragraph{Chrominance never separates from the prior, in any dataflow.} In all six
population-by-dataflow cells the chroma ratio interval includes $1$. We state the
declared rule's own output rather than only the conclusion we draw from it: at the
primary seed that rule marks a chroma task supported in four of the six cells---$C_b$
under \textsc{summed}-natural, \textsc{parallel}-control and \textsc{serial}-natural,
and both chroma tasks under \textsc{parallel}-natural. Every one of them passes on a
margin under five percent (ratios $0.951$ to $0.997$), which its interval cannot
separate from zero, and none carries a $\star$. We report them as unsupported and flag the rule, rather than
suppressing four verdicts that a reader recomputing the table would find. The contrast with
luma is best read from the intervals themselves rather than one side of them: control
luma lies between $20\%$ and $53\%$ tighter than the no-input prior, while control $C_b$
under \textsc{summed} lies between $5\%$ tighter and $11\%$ looser, and $C_r$ between
$7\%$ tighter and $16\%$ looser. Under \textsc{serial} the chroma sets are resolvably
\emph{worse}: $C_r$ has ratio $1.37$ with interval $[1.04, 1.63]$, entirely above $1$,
and neither chroma task is supported in a single one of the $20$ re-splits.

This agrees in direction with the mean-advantage result of \S\ref{sec:unc} but is a
weaker statement, and we do not let it pass for the stronger one. The two measure
different functionals. The bootstrap there compares \emph{mean} chroma error, where the
reconstruction is resolvably worse than the constant predictor ($-0.34$ $[-0.51,
-0.18]$); the ratio here compares the \emph{90th-percentile} error, where reconstruction
and prior are indistinguishable ($1.007$ $[0.95, 1.11]$). Both say no colour was
recovered; only the first says the attempt actively hurt.

\paragraph{Natural photographs fail on structure, and the coarse task confirms it.}
Natural-group $Y$ is supported in $5\%$ of re-splits under all three dataflows, at ratios
of $1.065$, $1.043$ and $1.052$---no better than emitting the dataset mean. The secondary
coarse task corroborates this independently and for free: $8\times8$ block-averaged $Y$
scores \emph{worse} than full resolution in every dataflow ($1.105$, $1.084$, $1.099$).
Coarse structure is the easiest thing a reconstructor can recover, so a method that
cannot beat the prior even on block means is not partially succeeding at fine detail. It
is contributing nothing.

\paragraph{The verdict is split-sensitive, and we report the sensitivity.} A table of
verdicts from one partition invites reading six findings where there are not six. Two
cases make the point. Control $C_b$ is supported in $15\%$ of re-splits under
\textsc{summed} and $50\%$ under \textsc{parallel}---the same task and population,
decided by the partition. Conversely, \textsc{parallel} control $Y$ has the tightest
ratio in the entire table ($0.530$) yet fails the declared rule at the primary seed,
because its realised coverage landed at $0.83$; it is supported in $90\%$ of re-splits.
We therefore treat the $\star$ column---ratio below $1$ under every re-split---as what
supports a claim, and a high support fraction alone as insufficient: natural $C_b$ is
supported in $80\%$ of re-splits on a margin of about $2\%$, which the interval cannot
separate from zero.

\paragraph{The calibration itself checks out.} Mean realised coverage across
\input{tables/val_ncells}\unskip{} cell--task combinations and $20$ re-splits is
\input{tables/val_coverage}\unskip{}, against the $k/(n+1) =
\input{tables/val_coverage_expected}\unskip{}$ expected for $k=91$, $n=100$. Had coverage
sat materially below that, the calibration and assessment halves would not be

\paragraph{Status of this analysis.} It is retrospective and we label it so. These
assessment rows are the same held-out examples that informed the corrections in
\S\ref{sec:corrections} and the claims above, and re-splitting previously inspected data
does not make it fresh: neither the frozen predictor nor the task definitions were chosen
independently of these cases. The coverage figures are therefore exploratory evidence
about the reporting formulation, not a prospective guarantee. Two components---the
interval on the radius ratio, and the re-split sweep---were added after seeing the first
output and are reported as post-hoc. The protocol fixed beforehand covers \textsc{summed}
only; the other two dataflows were run under the identical protocol as an extension.

\section{Corrections following external review}
\label{sec:corrections}

An external review of the repository at commit \texttt{d0eedd4} identified defects that
change what parts of this work can claim. We record them rather than silently repairing
them, because two of them invalidate results we had previously reported.

accumulator and splitting on source scene. The original ranking does not survive.}

\paragraph{Serial accumulator (simulator only).} The simulated serial datapath summed
across channels but retained only the last kernel tap of each cycle: for products
$1\ldots27$ it produced $[9,27,54]$ where the documented model gives $[45,171,378]$. The
existing test suite passed throughout, because it verified only that the separately
computed convolution output was unchanged---never the accumulator itself. The RTL was
always correct, so no measured result is affected; simulated serial results are.

\paragraph{Source-scene overlap in the Phase~0 pilot.} The pilot built sixteen rolled
copies of four photographs and split by array position, so every held-out row came from a
scene already present in training. Splitting on source scene inverts the result
(Table~\ref{tab:phase0}). With four scenes the corrected pilot is too small to support any
ranking, and we make none. The hardware evaluation is unaffected: all 2{,}000 rows per
group are distinct source images, so its row split was already scene-disjoint.

\paragraph{Swap penalty needs a prior reference.} As above, a constant predictor incurs a
channel-permutation penalty when channel distributions differ. We now report the excess
over the prior. Notably, our first attempt at this fix added the parameter to the function
signature without replacing the body; combined with a defaulting accessor in the reporting
code it printed a confident $0.000$ for every cell. The regression test now asserts that
the keys exist rather than defaulting.

\paragraph{Smaller repairs.} The shuffled control permuted rows across the whole dataset
rather than within each partition; PyTorch's generator was never seeded, so a rerun at
``seed 0'' was not reproducible; the anchor-validation driver selected whichever averaging
level best matched a fixed $\mathrm{avg}{=}1$ reference, which could mark a mismatched
simulation predictive; and our SSIM used uniform rather than Gaussian windows and is now
named \texttt{SSIM\_box11} to avoid implying comparability with library scores. Repairing
the shuffled control moved the headline advantage from $+14.02$ to $+14.19$, so that
conclusion is robust to it.

\section{Why: a measured instrumentation requirement}

\begin{table}[t]\centering\small
\caption{Reconstruction quality vs.\ training-set size (MSSIM). Natural is flat.}
\label{tab:scale}
\input{tables/tab_scaling}
\end{table}

We eliminated the non-instrumentation explanations by measurement. Training-set size is
flat for natural images from 500 to 2{,}000 (Table~\ref{tab:scale}) while the control
saturates by 500, so the $30\times$ shortfall against \cite{huegle2023}'s 60{,}000 images
is not the constraint. Averaging $16\times$ yields a measured $1.70\times$ SNR---not the
$4\times$ that $\sqrt{N}$ predicts---indicating a persistent component. Nine kernels gave
no chroma gain. Replacing our hand-picked sample window with a learned temporal filter
scored \emph{worse} (MSSIM $0.540$ vs $0.561$), indicating featurisation is not the
bottleneck.

The requirement follows from two measurements on the same bench. Natural images measure
$\rho=0.105$ between the measured feature and the noise-free power model; the control
group measures $\rho=0.523$ \emph{and demonstrably reconstructs}. Reaching a
demonstrably-sufficient SNR therefore requires $5.8\times$, of which averaging supplies
$1.70\times$, leaving $3.4\times$ for instrumentation.

Sampling rate is the specific deficiency. Correlation as a function of ADC samples
averaged per dwell window peaks at three samples ($\rho=0.333$) and falls to $0.188$ over
all 32: only ${\sim}3$ samples land on the data-dependent transient, because at
$105$\,MS/s and a $5$\,MHz clock the transient is sub-cycle. Measured intra-window
residual autocorrelation leaves ${\sim}14$ effectively independent samples per 32, so
noise does average down with more samples---there are simply none to average.
Wei et~al.\ used $2.5$\,GS/s, which their \S5 per-cycle RC curve-fitting requires; at
${\sim}500$ samples per cycle we estimate ${\sim}10\times$ SNR, above the $3.4\times$
needed. \emph{This last step is an estimate from measured inputs, not itself measured.}

\subsection{The pipeline can fit; the failure is not implementation}

A reconstruction that scores below the analytic prior admits several explanations:
an implementation defect, mispaired inputs, bad scaling, insufficient optimization,
poor generalization, or an input that simply carries too little information. The first
four are excluded by showing the pipeline can memorise a small, correctly-paired
problem, so we ran that check with a predeclared budget of 400 epochs on eight images
(Table~\ref{tab:overfit}).

All three arms fit. The synthetic control reaches MSSIM $0.984$ with the loss falling to
$0.0000$, so model, loss, scaling and pairing are functional. The natural arm---the case
that fails on held-out data---memorises eight images to MSSIM $0.986$ with a prediction
spread of $0.91\times$ the targets', i.e.\ it emits genuinely distinct outputs per input
rather than collapsing to the mean.

Implementation, pairing, scaling and optimization are therefore not the explanation for
the natural-image result. What remains is generalization or the information content of
the input, and this test deliberately does not separate those two: memorising eight
images is a functionality check, not evidence of recovery. The value of running it is
narrower and specific---it converts the natural-image result from an inconclusive model
failure into a bounded negative.

We validated our bench simulation by replaying a capture whose measured outcome was
known. Modelling bench noise as i.i.d.\ Gaussian was measurably pessimistic
(Table~\ref{tab:anchor}). The measured residual is strongly correlated---$+0.59$ at
lag~1 across windows---and white noise is the hardest case for a learned reconstructor.
Substituting \emph{real measured residuals} for a parametric model closed 60\% of the
gap. We recommend this decomposition-and-replay approach generally: it costs one existing
capture and converts a simulation from suggestive to predictive.

\section{Limitations}

The Phase~0 simulation is not predictive of dataflow ranking and we do not use it as
such. Single device, single bitstream, single deployed kernel. CIFAR-10 at $32\times32$ is not
representative of all natural imagery. Profiling and evaluation share a distribution, a
limitation \cite{wei2018} also notes. Our claim is bounded to this line-buffer geometry; all three dataflows were captured,
but a systolic or output-stationary accelerator may leak differently. The $10\times$ SNR projection for a faster oscilloscope is an
extrapolation. We have not tested electromagnetic acquisition, which would address
signal quality but not temporal resolution, since the ADC rate is unchanged.

The calibrated analysis of \S\ref{sec:calibrated} carries its own limits. It is
retrospective: its assessment examples are the same rows that informed earlier claims
here, so its coverage figures are exploratory evidence about the reporting formulation
and not a prospective guarantee, which would need examples with a clean usage history.
Its guarantee is marginal, not conditional---it bounds task error at the stated rate
over the population and says nothing about any individual image, nor about a subgroup
chosen after the fact. The per-task levels are separate, so the table must not be read
as a joint statement. All captures come from one session, and no session variable
exists in these artifacts, so cross-session transport cannot be assessed at all. Scene
and source image coincide in this dataset, which is a property of this capture rather
than a general one: re-photographed or augmented variants would break the assumption
that makes the split disjoint.

\section{Conclusion}

We recover recognisable image structure from RGB-accelerator power traces and no colour,
under controls that exclude both scene complexity and prior-driven colourisation. The
honest description is \emph{grayscale recovery from an RGB-input accelerator}. That
reading was pre-registered before the measurements existed, not fitted to them.

The pre-registration covers the reading, not the calibrated reformulation that now
bounds it. \S\ref{sec:calibrated} was written after the measurements and rescores rows
already inspected; its coverage figures are exploratory, and we mark them so wherever
they appear. What it adds is not a new measurement but a stricter accounting of the
existing one---per-task, against a baseline calibrated the same way, with the
sensitivity of each verdict to the calibration split reported beside it.

We suggest the transferable contributions are the control set and this reporting
formulation, rather than the reconstruction. Neither foundational paper reports a prior-only baseline, which means
neither can separate ``recovered the input'' from ``reproduced the dataset.'' Our first
attempt at that baseline was itself broken in a way that inflated our apparent result
$68\times$, and only an analytic cross-check caught it. For a field whose headline
artifact is a convincing image, we think a baseline that a reader can verify in closed
form is worth more than a sharper picture.
