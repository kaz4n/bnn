Condition-Transfer Gaps in Power Input Recovery

Four openings in FPGA power side-channel input reconstruction, ranked by the evidence I can already put behind them rather than by how large they sound.

Tarek Alhafez CW305 · Artix-7 XC7A100T · ChipWhisperer-Lite 10 papers reviewed Gap 3 resolved · Gap 1 thermal-checked

Every gap below is one I can support with a measurement already on disk, and each is named as open by at least one of the papers reviewed. Ranking is by strength of existing evidence, not ambition.

Gap 3 is now answered, and answering it revised the other entries. The candidate search radius carried over from the original paper turned out to be badly wrong for this hardware: tuning it moved the project's headline from F1 0.843 to 0.969. Where a result still has a confound I cannot remove, the confound is stated and the experiment that would remove it is named. That is the useful part of a document like this.

Operating-condition transfer for input reconstruction

Profiled power attacks assume the attacker profiles and attacks under the same conditions. The literature has tested that assumption for key recovery and for classification, but not for input reconstruction — which is the attack class that actually recovers private data.

Reibold, Meyers & Tahoori, HOST 2026 — "Talking Traces" recovers spoken audio from an FPGA NN accelerator. Their practical-challenges section names the gap outright: differences between profiling and victim setup "may affect the observed power traces due to measurement setup, environmental conditions, or manufacturing variations," and these "could be addressed in future work by collecting profiling traces under varying conditions."
Glamočanin et al., DATE 2023 — temperature does move remote power SCA success, and ignoring it yields "misleading and overly optimistic" ML profiling results. Scope is CPA and workload classification, not reconstruction. Their key qualifier lands squarely on my setup: the effect matters most "when the leakage is limited (e.g., a small victim circuit)."

I ran the varying-conditions experiment they name. One template, 40 profiling images, attacked against the same 40 evaluation images at two clocks. The result is not the symmetric degradation I expected.

The finding that survives δ tuning is the asymmetry. Profiling low and attacking high collapses to near the all-background baseline and gets worse as δ tightens (0.081 to 0.039). Profiling high and attacking low keeps working (0.873). A template is bound to the clock it was profiled at, and the binding is one-directional.

Two claims I made at δ = 1.0 did not survive, and both corrections matter more than the original claims.

Corrected: "the 10 MHz template is best at both clocks"

True at δ = 1.0 (0.803 against 0.781), false once δ is tuned. At δ = 0.3 the 5 MHz template wins its own clock decisively, 0.931 against 0.873. Matched-condition profiling is best on its own clock; what the high-clock template buys is coverage of both, not superiority at either.

Falsified: my explanation for why mixing loses

I claimed the mixed template loses because its 5 MHz rows contribute wrong candidates that still fall inside δ. That predicts mixing should improve as δ tightens. It does the opposite — 0.453 down to 0.259 at 10 MHz. The likelier explanation is one my experiment design confounded: the mixed template holds only 20 images of 10 MHz coverage against the pure template's 40. A tighter radius punishes thin coverage. So my 20+20 split conflated mixing conditions with halving per-condition coverage, and the honest reading is that mixing has not yet been tested cleanly at all.

Ruling out the thermal explanation

All the numbers above came from sequential profiling: forty images at 5 MHz, then forty at 10 MHz. That is precisely the acquisition pattern the DATE 2023 work warns about, and its caveat — the effect is largest when the leakage is weak — describes this setup exactly. A referee would be right to ask whether the cross-clock collapse is thermal drift between two acquisition blocks rather than anything electrical.

So I recaptured the same forty profiling images with the two clocks interleaved: every image taken at both conditions back to back, so the slow thermal ramp of the session lands on both in the same proportion. Both conditions passed the functional check with 676/676 outputs bit-exact.

The largest discrepancy anywhere in the matrix is 0.039 F1. The asymmetry it would have to explain is 0.799 — 0.868 against 0.069. Thermal drift therefore accounts for at most about five per cent of the effect, and the transfer failure is electrical. Note also the direction: interleaving made the cross-clock case slightly better, so the sequential captures had been overstating the collapse rather than inventing it.

This is the check that lets Gap 01 be written up. Without it the whole result rests on an acquisition protocol that a reviewer of the temperature paper would reject on sight.

What the experiment should have been

Hold per-condition image count constant, not the total: 40 images at each clock against 40 at one, with δ tuned separately for each template since they differ in size. Then add frequencies — 5, 7.5, 10, 20 MHz, all profile/attack pairs — to test whether the asymmetry is monotone in frequency or specific to this pair. 7.5 MHz captures already exist and the capture path runs 20 MHz cleanly.

Input quantisation as a leakage variable

Both foundational works in this line attack a first layer that receives grayscale pixels. Wei et al. multiply real 0–255 pixels by binary weights; Moini et al. state plainly that "the input to the BNN is a grayscale" image and that every layer except the first receives binary. Nobody has held the hardware constant and varied the input bit-width to see what that choice costs an attacker.

Application-Specific Power Side-Channel Attacks & Countermeasures, arXiv 2512.23785 (Dec 2025) — names the gap from the defence side: existing countermeasures "lack adaptation to ML workloads, where model dynamics and data-dependent sparsity create unique leakage patterns," and calls for "ML-specific leakage models that capture sparsity, pruning, and quantization effects."

I built two bitstreams that differ only in the input datapath — one thresholds the pixel to a bit before the convolution, the other multiplies the real 0–255 pixel by binary weights — and attacked both with the same generator, the same split and the same number of images.

Removing the binarisation did not merely make the comparison with the paper fairer, it made the attack better — mean error under 4 % of full scale and 0.945 correlation with the true image. The framing that follows is a defensive one, and it is the part worth publishing: input binarisation degrades reconstruction but does not prevent it. A defender who quantises the input aggressively buys a real but partial reduction in what an attacker recovers, and that trade has never been quantified on one platform.

The confound a referee will raise first

The two designs are not only different in input width. The grey build is materially different silicon: 15,908 LUTs against 2,990, 11,158 flops against 1,574, and a 72-bit leakage-amplifier word against 9-bit. The improvement could come from richer input information or from a bigger, differently routed circuit with a wider toggling bank.

Experiment that would separate them

A third build that breaks the correlation: either a grey datapath whose amplifier rides only a thresholded term, or a binary datapath given the full 72-bit amplifier width. Either isolates input information from circuit size, and both are a day of work since the RTL is parameterised already.

The search radius must scale with template size

This was the cheapest experiment on the page, so I ran it first. It is answered, and it moved every other number in this document.

Going from 40 to 300 profiling images at the published δ = 1.0 made the attack worse (F1 0.843 to 0.759), which is what raised the question. Sweeping δ over thirteen values per template shows why: both curves are unimodal, and both peak far below the published value.

The published δ was costing this project 0.13 F1. The 40-image template peaks at δ = 0.25 with F1 0.958; the 300-image template peaks at δ = 0.06 with F1 0.969 and MSSIM 0.961. Recognition is 40/40 across the whole central range. The headline result of the project moves from 0.843 to 0.969.

So more profiling data does help — but only if δ shrinks with it. Roughly, 7.5× the template rows wants 4× the tighter radius. At any fixed δ the larger template looks strictly worse, which is the trap I fell into, and which anyone reporting reconstruction quality against profiling-set size will fall into unless they co-tune.

Two points is not an exponent

Two template sizes give a direction, not a scaling law. Sizes between 40 and 300, and past 300, would show whether the relation is a clean power law or merely monotone.

Probe-kernel mismatch as an attacker-capability bound

Profiled attacks on NN accelerators quietly assume the attacker profiles with the kernels the victim actually deploys. I tested the assumption by profiling with one-hot probe kernels and attacking traces captured while the FPGA ran the real trained kernels.

The attack does not merely weaken, it drops below the all-background baseline: the reconstruction actively paints foreground where there is none. That is a sharper statement than "profiling matters" and it bounds the threat model — an attacker who cannot learn the deployed weights is worse off than one who guesses blank.

It also argues that weight confidentiality is an input-privacy control, which connects a model-extraction defence to an input-reconstruction defence. Those two literatures are usually kept apart.

Two connections worth one experiment each

Frequency scaling as a free defence

Hertzbleed (Wang et al., USENIX Security 2022) turned data-dependent DVFS frequency into a remote timing channel. My clock result points the other way: because a power template is bound to the clock it was profiled at, DVFS is already a partial defence against template reconstruction. A defender who varies the accelerator clock during inference invalidates the attacker's template for free. Nobody appears to have evaluated deliberate clock jitter as an input-reconstruction countermeasure, and it would cost far less than the dual-rail hiding or dynamic bitstream swapping the countermeasure papers propose.

For scale of alternative: FPGA-Patch swaps isofunctional bitstream variants at run time for 14.2 % area overhead, and the dual-hiding asynchronous-logic AES accelerator reaches >20M traces to disclosure through dual-rail mapping. Both are heavy compared with changing a clock.

My own acquisition protocol is not the recommended one

The DATE 2023 temperature work insists on interleaved trace acquisition so thermal drift spreads evenly across classes; acquiring each class consecutively lets a model learn temperature instead of leakage. My captures are sequential. Given their finding that the effect is largest exactly where leakage is weak — my diluted small-victim case — some part of my condition-transfer result could be thermal rather than electrical. Re-running the clock matrix with interleaved acquisition is a cheap robustness check I should do before writing Gap 01 up.

What stays out

One earlier result of mine is void and must not appear in any comparison: a 9,000-image generative reconstruction run whose capture predates my authenticity gate. It records no design check and no functional check, and its bitstream hash does not match the verified one, so there is no evidence the FPGA was running my design rather than the stock AES example when those traces were taken. Its numbers looked good, which is precisely why they are dangerous.

The same caution applies to the "FPGA reprogrammed" condition in my 30 August report. Reprogramming a CW305 whose FPGA is already configured is a silent no-op — PROGRAM_B never clears the device — so that row measures the same configuration later in time, not a reconfiguration. Withdrawn until re-run across a real power cycle.

Order I would work in

The first item is done; the rest are in the order I would take them, cheapest and most confound-removing first.

  1. δ sweep — done, 1 Sept. Twenty-seven attack runs, no board needed. Moved the headline from 0.843 to 0.969 and forced two corrections above.
  2. Interleaved re-capture of the clock matrix — done, 1 Sept. The asymmetry survives interleaved acquisition intact; thermal drift accounts for at most 0.039 F1 against an effect of 0.799. Gap 01 is electrical.
  3. The mixing experiment done properly — equal per-condition coverage, δ tuned per template. The current mixed result measures the wrong thing.
  4. Four-frequency transfer grid. Converts the asymmetry from a two-point observation into a rule. This is the paper.
  5. Third bitstream for the quantisation confound. Built and timing-clean: the binary datapath with the grey design's 72-bit amplifier. Needs a capture run.
  6. Clock jitter as a countermeasure — the defensive companion, and the result most likely to interest a hardware-security venue.
